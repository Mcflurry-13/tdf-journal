<Entry date="TBD" title="Concept · A Physical Forecast">

Sunset in Bloom is an ambient display that turns Berkeley's sunset-quality forecast into movement and light. A positional MG90S servo moves a wire linkage to open the petals, while two RGB NeoPixel sticks illuminate the flower. The aim is to make a number noticeable as a small physical change.

The Adafruit Feather ESP32 V2 controls the outputs. After direct HTTPS requests on the campus network failed, the data request moved to a Python service on the Mac, which sends a validated forecast over USB. This separated internet access from physical control; the browser makes the relationship between the forecast and the flower visible.

<Entry date="TBD" title="Motion · Printed Petals">

We began with two thin, 3D-printed petal layers joined near their tips. Pulling their centers apart opens the flower. The lower layer has cutouts that allow its stems to bend; its center was later revised into a round hub.

We tested the pull by hand, then placed the flower upright with the servo below and to one side. This let us work out the movement before building the enclosure.

<Entry date="TBD" title="Material · Testing the Bend">

We also tried laser-cut wood, using repeated cuts to make the petals more flexible. In the bending tests, the wood still could not curve enough for the opening motion, so we set that route aside and continued with the printed petals. The useful comparison was how far the material could move, not just how the flat outline looked.

<Entry date="TBD" title="Fabrication · Light Through the Form">

A cardboard box let us test the whole mechanism. We cut a slot in its side to hold the servo, keeping the horn outside so it could pull the wire toward the flower.

The enclosure then evolved into a round shell. Its flowing openings echo the petals and let the warm light spill out, connecting the form to the sunset theme. A removable lid gives access to the mechanism; a rigid bridge supports the flower and LEDs, while a separate diffuser softens the light.

<Entry date="2026-10-15" title="Testing · Isolate, Then Reconnect">

To check the MG90S independently, I disconnected the LEDs and flower linkage and used short pulse tests, followed by a repeating sweep. Serial output confirmed that commands were running, but the motor sometimes stopped; moving a wire could make it work again. That pointed to an intermittent connection rather than a missing command.

I redistributed the crowded power and ground connections, repaired the LED wiring and tested the sticks on their own. After the light test worked, I added the servo back and confirmed that both outputs moved together. Separating these tests helped me distinguish software, wiring and mechanical problems.

<Entry date="2026-10-15" title="Control · Forecast and Motion">

Hold translates the sunset-quality score into a still opening. During roughly a week of testing, the new forecast samples mostly stayed below 60. Using the full 0–100 range made those everyday differences less visible, so we mapped 60 to full opening. This makes the score-to-motion relationship clearer in a demonstration; scores above 60 share the same maximum.

Breathe demonstrates motion independently of the score. It shook at the highest point, so we reduced only its upper limit to 150°, keeping Hold’s mapping intact. Each movement takes three seconds, followed by a 1.5-second pause. Color follows forecast quality in both modes, and the light brightens as the flower opens.

<Entry date="2026-10-15" title="Interface · Reading the Forecast">

We built the frontend to make the connection between today's sunset quality and the flower's movement easier to read. The score sits beside an animated flower, so a visitor can relate the number to its opening and light at a glance.

Hold shows the forecast as a still position; Breathe makes the motion easy to demonstrate; Stop ends the output. The animation illustrates the commanded movement rather than measuring the physical petals. This embedded version uses our recorded October 15 sample so the interface can be explored in the journal.
